The Thesis

Distance from a city can shape your future.

Across the European Union, predominantly rural regions often report weaker outcomes than urban ones: lower employment, lower tertiary education attainment, and in some cases shorter life expectancy. The pattern is not universal, and it is not explained by transport alone. But it does follow a familiar geography: regions closer to major cities and transport corridors tend to have more direct access to jobs, universities, healthcare, and public services.

That is why this project looks beyond simple country rankings. The question is not whether education levels are higher in the Netherlands than in Hungary, or whether richer countries perform better than poorer ones. The more revealing question is how large the gaps are within each country.

In countries with dense and reliable transport networks, regional differences can be less pronounced. In countries where connectivity is more uneven, the distance between a capital region and a peripheral region can become a much larger divide.

The Evidence

Rail density is not the whole story, but it reveals part of the pattern.

Using Eurostat regional data across {{ stats.n_regions }} NUTS-2 regions and {{ stats.n_countries }} EU member states, this project compares outcomes in employment, education, health, economic performance, and transport infrastructure. For connectivity, it uses railway density as a proxy: kilometres of railway line per 1,000 km².

That proxy has limits. It does not measure train frequency, ticket prices, service reliability, travel time to a university or hospital, or whether a railway line actually serves the people who live nearby. But it does show where fixed transport infrastructure is concentrated, and where it is comparatively thin.

The pattern is not a simple rule where every connected region performs better than every isolated one. National wealth, urban concentration, age structure, and education systems all matter. Still, the data points to a recurring divide: regions with denser transport infrastructure are more often part of the economic and institutional core, while less connected regions are more likely to sit at the edge of opportunity.

The Stakes

Funding alone cannot close the distance.

The EU has set aside around €392 billion for Cohesion Policy in 2021-2027 , with regional development and infrastructure among its central priorities. That spending matters. But the persistence of regional gaps shows that money alone does not guarantee equal access to jobs, education, healthcare, or public services.

The question is not simply whether investment reaches poorer regions. It is whether those investments make everyday access easier: whether people can reach a university without leaving their region, commute to work without depending on a car, or get to essential services without planning their day around a limited timetable.

The data pages that follow do not prescribe a single solution. They show where opportunity is concentrated, where connectivity is thinner, and where regional inequality remains visible in the numbers. The issue is not just how much Europe spends, but what kind of access that spending creates.
